On the bench in front of me is a Sanyo RP 1250, bought for twelve dollars at a Rozelle market. The cabinet is cracked at one corner, the dial glass has a hairline fracture running from nine o'clock to the tuning knob, and it hasn't made a sound since sometime in the 1970s.

I have been looking at it for three weeks.

There is something about the repair of old things that resists hurry. You can't talk yourself into understanding a circuit by reading faster. The oscillator is either drifting or it isn't, and finding out which requires sitting with a multimeter and a schematic for longer than feels comfortable, paying a quality of attention that modern work rarely asks of you.

> The amateur does not ask what something is worth. She asks what it would mean to make it work again.

Richard Sennett writes in *The Craftsman* that tacit knowledge — the kind that lives in the hands rather than the head — can only be acquired slowly, through repetition with variation. You don't learn to centre clay by reading about clay. You learn it by failing at it, noticing how you failed, and trying again with that particular failure in mind.

Transistor radios are like this. The schematic tells you where the signal should go. It doesn't tell you that a particular batch of germanium transistors from 1968 had a known tendency to develop leakage between collector and base as they aged, or that the original designer had compensated for this with a bias resistor that now reads high because it absorbed moisture from forty years in a garage. That knowledge comes from having handled enough of them, from other restorers' notes on forums, from following a thread of inference through a piece of equipment until it gives up what's wrong.

This is slow work. It is also, I have come to think, the point.
